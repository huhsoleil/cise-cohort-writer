**Abstract** {S1b}

Purpose: Optimal distalization and lateralization after reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) remain unresolved. We hypothesized that the distalization shoulder angle (DSA) and lateralization shoulder angle (LSA) would show nonlinear associations with 2-year American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) scores and that greater DSA would increase the risk of acromial or scapular spine stress fracture.

Methods: This multicenter retrospective cohort study included 240 of 300 patients who underwent primary RTSA at 4 centers in Korea between 2016 and 2022. Two blinded raters measured DSA and LSA on postoperative radiographs. Associations were estimated with restricted cubic splines in multivariable models adjusted for prespecified confounders, and missing data were handled by multiple imputation (m=20). Raw data are available as Dataset 1.

Results: Inter-rater reliability was excellent (intraclass correlation coefficients, 0.953 and 0.937). Over a median follow-up of 39.0 months, both angles showed inverted U-shaped associations with the 2-year ASES score (P for nonlinearity=0.005 for DSA and 0.002 for LSA), with adjusted maxima near 52° and 87°, respectively. Compared with the median LSA, an LSA of 76.2° was associated with a 4.9-point lower ASES score (95% confidence interval [CI], −8.0 to −1.8). Stress fractures occurred in 16 patients (6.7%); each 10° increase in DSA was associated with an adjusted odds ratio of 4.07 (95% CI, 2.07–8.00), and the standardized risk rose from 1.0% at 40° to 14.7% at 60°.

Conclusion: Intermediate distalization and lateralization were associated with better function, whereas greater distalization was associated with a higher stress-fracture risk.

Keywords: Arthroplasty, replacement, shoulder; Cohort studies; Fractures, stress; Radiography; Treatment outcome; Republic of Korea

**Introduction**

Background/rationale {S2}

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) restores elevation in cuff-deficient shoulders by lowering and medializing the center of rotation, and contemporary implants allow surgeons to vary both distalization and lateralization of the humerus. Boutsiadis et al. [1] proposed the distalization shoulder angle (DSA) and lateralization shoulder angle (LSA) as reproducible radiographic surrogates and suggested optimal ranges for function. Subsequent single-center studies, however, reported weak or absent correlations with patient-reported outcomes [2], and the acromion–greater tuberosity distance appeared to have a nonlinear, intermediate optimum [3]. An editorial in this journal called the optimal amount of distalization and lateralization an unresolved question [4]. At the same time, excessive distalization may increase tension on the deltoid origin and the risk of acromial and scapular spine stress fractures, a complication associated with both implant positioning and patient factors such as female sex and osteoporosis [5-7]. Previous studies mostly analyzed these angles as linear terms or dichotomized them, assessed function and fracture risk separately, and rarely reported measurement reliability or handled missing follow-up data explicitly.

Objectives {S3}

This study aimed to estimate the associations of postoperative DSA and LSA with 2-year functional outcome and with acromial or scapular spine stress fracture after primary RTSA. We hypothesized that (1) DSA and LSA would show inverted U-shaped associations with the 2-year ASES score and (2) greater DSA would be associated with a higher risk of stress fracture, independent of sex, age, and osteoporosis.

**Methods**

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating hospitals ([Hospital A] IRB No. [XXXX-XX-XXX]; [Hospital B] IRB No. [XXXX-XX-XXX]; [Hospitals C and D] IRB Nos. [ ]). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design using de-identified records. Data were anonymized at each center before pooling.

Study design {S4}

This was a multicenter retrospective cohort study. The exposure was postoperative radiographic distalization and lateralization, and the outcomes were the 2-year ASES score and stress fracture during follow-up. The study is reported according to the STROBE statement for cohort studies [12,13].

Setting {S5}

Four tertiary referral hospitals in Korea contributed consecutive patients who underwent primary RTSA between January 2016 and December 2022. Follow-up data were collected from medical records until December 2025, so that every patient had the opportunity for at least 24 months of follow-up.

Participants {S6a}

All adults who underwent primary RTSA during the study period were screened (n=300). Exclusion criteria were revision arthroplasty, RTSA for acute proximal humerus fracture (<3 weeks), tumor resection, infection or staged procedures, age <50 years, and absence of an adequate postoperative Grashey radiograph. Patients were followed at 6 weeks, 3, 6, and 12 months, and annually thereafter according to each center's routine.

Variables {S7}

The primary exposures were DSA and LSA measured on the first postoperative true anteroposterior (Grashey) radiograph [1]. Secondary exposures were the acromion–greater tuberosity distance (AGTD) and the distalization–lateralization index (DLI), calculated as the square root of the sum of the squared acromiohumeral interval and acromiohumeral offset. The primary outcomes were the ASES score at 2 years and acromial or scapular spine stress fracture (Levy type I–III) diagnosed during follow-up. Secondary outcomes were active forward elevation and external rotation at 2 years, achievement of the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the ASES score, and reoperation. For illustration we used an MCID of 20.9 points; implant-, diagnosis-, and sex-specific thresholds have been reported [8]. Potential confounders were selected a priori as factors plausibly affecting both implant positioning and outcome: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), diagnosis (cuff tear arthropathy, massive irreparable cuff tear, osteoarthritis with cuff deficiency, fracture sequelae), humeral implant design (inlay or onlay), preoperative ASES score, and center. Osteoporosis (chart diagnosis or dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry [DXA] T-score) was added to the fracture model.

Data sources/measurement {S8}

Demographic, clinical, and implant data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized case report form (Dataset 1; codebook in Supplement 1). ASES scores were collected by clinic staff not involved in this analysis. Two fellowship-trained surgeons independently measured DSA, LSA, and AGTD on digital radiographs using the definitions of Boutsiadis et al. [1]; the mean of the 2 raters was used for analysis. Inter-rater reliability was assessed with the 2-way random-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measure intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC[2,1]) [9]. Stress fractures were diagnosed on radiographs or computed tomography and classified by the treating surgeon.

Bias {S9}

To reduce selection bias, consecutive patients were screened at every center. Radiographic raters were blinded to clinical outcomes and to each other's measurements, limiting information bias. Confounding was addressed by multivariable adjustment for prespecified covariates and by including center in all models. Attrition bias was addressed by comparing patients who were lost to follow-up with those retained and by multiple imputation that included predictors of dropout.

Study size {S10}

The study size was determined by the number of eligible patients at the 4 centers. A post hoc calculation based on the primary outcome showed that 240 patients provided 80% power at a 2-sided α of 0.05 to detect an effect size f² of 0.058 (an R² increase of about 0.04) attributable to the 6 spline terms for DSA and LSA in a model with 21 parameters (F test, noncentral F distribution). For the stress-fracture outcome, the expected number of events was small; we therefore restricted the fracture model to 4 parameters and report the events-per-parameter ratio.

Statistical methods {S12}

(a) Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation and categorical variables as number (%). DSA and LSA were modeled with restricted cubic splines with 4 knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles [11]. The ASES score was analyzed with multivariable linear regression including both spline-transformed angles and the confounders listed above. Nonlinearity was tested with a Wald test of the nonlinear spline terms. Results are expressed as adjusted differences in ASES score at the 10th, 25th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of each angle relative to its median. Stress fracture was analyzed with logistic regression including DSA (per 10°), sex, age, and osteoporosis; nonlinearity of DSA was checked with spline terms. Standardized (marginal) risks at DSA values of 40° and 60° were obtained by averaging model predictions over the cohort, with 95% CIs from 1,000 bootstrap samples.

(b) No subgroup or interaction analyses were prespecified or performed, because the sample size was insufficient to estimate interactions with spline terms reliably.

(c) Missing values in the ASES score, range of motion, BMI, preoperative ASES score, and DXA T-score were handled by multiple imputation by chained equations with 20 imputed datasets [10]. The imputation model included all analysis variables, the outcomes, and auxiliary variables (American Society of Anesthesiologists class, chart diagnosis of osteoporosis, center). Estimates were combined using Rubin's rules.

(d) Patients lost to follow-up before 24 months remained in the cohort; their baseline characteristics were compared with those of patients who completed follow-up, and their missing outcomes were imputed.

(e) Sensitivity analyses comprised (1) complete-case analysis, (2) exposures measured by rater 1 only, (3) exclusion of patients with fracture sequelae, (4) additional adjustment for surgery year, (5) center as a random intercept in a linear mixed model, and (6) replacement of chart osteoporosis with the imputed DXA T-score in the fracture model.

Analyses were performed with Python ver. 3.13 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) and statsmodels ver. 0.15 [14]. Two-sided P<0.05 was considered significant. The analysis code is provided as Supplement 2.

**Results**

Participants {S13}

Of 300 screened patients, 60 were excluded (revision arthroplasty, 17; no adequate postoperative Grashey radiograph, 16; acute fracture, 13; age <50 years, 6; infection or staged procedure, 5; tumor resection, 3), leaving 240 patients from 4 centers (81, 58, 61, and 40 patients) (Fig. 1). Overall, 207 patients (86.3%) completed at least 24 months of follow-up and 33 were lost to follow-up. Patients lost to follow-up were older (73.7 vs. 70.8 years, P=0.024) and more often had American Society of Anesthesiologists class III (48.5% vs. 25.6%, P=0.013) than those retained, whereas DSA (48.3° vs. 48.6°, P=0.885) and LSA (88.1° vs. 87.2°, P=0.589) were similar. All 240 patients were included in the primary analysis. The raw data are available in Dataset 1.

Descriptive data {S14}

The mean age was 71.2±6.9 years, 143 patients (59.6%) were women, and cuff tear arthropathy was the most frequent diagnosis (148, 61.7%) (Table 1). The mean DSA was 48.5°±10.1° and the mean LSA was 87.3°±8.6°. Patients in the highest DSA tertile more often received onlay implants (68.4% vs. 37.5% in the lowest tertile). Data were missing for BMI (n=8), preoperative ASES score (n=20), DXA T-score (n=73), 2-year ASES score (n=33), and 2-year external rotation (n=41). The median follow-up was 39.0 months (interquartile range, 28.4–52.5), with 813.6 person-years in total.

Inter-rater reliability was excellent for DSA (ICC, 0.953; 95% CI, 0.939–0.962), LSA (ICC, 0.937; 95% CI, 0.918–0.951), and AGTD (ICC, 0.949; 95% CI, 0.934–0.961) (Table 2).

Outcome data {S15}

Among the 207 patients with 2-year data, the mean ASES score improved from 36.1±13.4 preoperatively to 72.2±12.7, and 85.1% of the 194 patients with both scores achieved the MCID. Stress fractures occurred in 16 patients (6.7%; Levy type I, 6; type II, 4; type III, 6) at a median of 5.4 months after surgery. The risk was 0/80 (0.0%), 4/81 (4.9%), and 12/79 (15.2%) in the lowest, middle, and highest DSA tertiles (boundaries, 44.4° and 53.0°) (Table 4).

Main results {S16}

Both angles were nonlinearly associated with the 2-year ASES score after adjustment (P for nonlinearity=0.005 for DSA and 0.002 for LSA) (Fig. 2, Table 3). Adjusted ASES scores were highest at a DSA of approximately 52° and an LSA of approximately 87°. Compared with the median LSA (87.4°), the adjusted ASES score was 4.9 points lower at the 10th percentile (76.2°; 95% CI, −8.0 to −1.8) and 2.7 points lower at the 90th percentile (97.0°; 95% CI, −5.3 to −0.03). Compared with the median DSA (49.0°), the score was 6.3 points lower at the 10th percentile (34.9°; 95% CI, −9.9 to −2.6), whereas the decrease at the 90th percentile (60.7°) was not statistically significant (−2.2; 95% CI, −5.3 to 0.8). Unadjusted estimates were similar (Table 3).

Each 10° increase in DSA was associated with a higher odds of stress fracture before (odds ratio [OR], 3.95; 95% CI, 2.04–7.63) and after adjustment for sex, age, and osteoporosis (OR, 4.07; 95% CI, 2.07–8.00; P<0.001), with no evidence of nonlinearity (P=0.642). The standardized risk of stress fracture was 1.0% (95% CI, 0.2–2.5) at a DSA of 40° and 14.7% (95% CI, 7.8–22.9) at 60°, an absolute risk difference of 13.6 percentage points (95% CI, 6.8–21.8) (Fig. 3).

Other analyses {S17}

The nonlinear association between LSA and ASES score was consistent across all sensitivity analyses (Table 5). The estimate at the 10th percentile of LSA ranged from −4.3 (exclusion of fracture sequelae) to −6.0 (rater 1 only). Using the imputed DXA T-score instead of chart osteoporosis yielded a similar fracture OR per 10° of DSA (3.70; 95% CI, 1.88–7.27).

**Discussion**

Key results {S18}

In this multicenter cohort, intermediate distalization (DSA about 52°) and lateralization (LSA about 87°) were associated with the best 2-year ASES scores, whereas low values of either angle were associated with clinically small to moderate reductions. Greater distalization was associated with a steep, approximately log-linear increase in stress-fracture risk.

Interpretation {S20}

The findings suggest a trade-off: moderate distalization may improve deltoid efficiency and function, but beyond the functional optimum further distalization adds fracture risk without functional gain. Because the differences in ASES score were below commonly cited MCID thresholds [8], the functional association should be regarded as modest, and multiple outcomes were examined without formal adjustment for multiplicity.

Comparison with previous studies

Our inverted U-shaped association agrees with the optimal ranges proposed by Boutsiadis et al. [1] and with the intermediate optimum reported for AGTD [3], but differs from the absence of association in a recent single-center study that used linear regression [2]. The association between greater distalization and stress fracture is consistent with multicenter and meta-analytic evidence [5-7].

Limitations {S19}

First, residual confounding by indication is possible because surgeons chose implant design and positioning according to patient anatomy; onlay designs were more common in the highest DSA tertile. If surgeons distalized more in shoulders with poorer bone or deltoid quality, the fracture association would be overestimated. Second, 33 patients (13.8%) lacked 2-year outcomes; dropouts were older and sicker, and if their outcomes were worse than predicted by the imputation model, the functional association could be biased toward the null. Third, only 16 fractures occurred (4 events per model parameter), so the fracture OR is imprecise and may be overestimated; penalized regression should be considered in larger datasets [11]. Fourth, angles were measured on 2-dimensional radiographs, and measurement error, although small (ICC >0.93), would attenuate associations.

Generalizability {S21}

The cohort comprised Korean patients treated at tertiary centers with a range of implant designs, so the results may apply to similar populations; generalizability to populations with different body size, bone density, or implant philosophies requires confirmation.

**Suggestions**

Prospective multicenter studies with standardized 3-dimensional measurement and implant-specific analyses are needed. Surgeons may consider avoiding excessive distalization, particularly in women and patients with osteoporosis.

**Conclusion**

Intermediate postoperative distalization and lateralization were associated with the best 2-year function after RTSA, and greater distalization was associated with a markedly higher risk of acromial or scapular spine stress fracture. These findings answer both study hypotheses and may help balance function and fracture risk when planning implant position.
